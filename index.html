What the sheet does. It reproduces, line for line, the in-flight fuel quantity check every company pilot must carry out (Part A 6.4.5.2) and the FCOM top-of-descent method (8.5.3). The minimum useable fuel at engine start (Part A 6.4.4.1) is produced by the company flight-planning software (Part A 6.4.2(a)) and is not repeated here — bring the planned FOB, the alternate fuel and the final reserve from the EFB nav log. Part A 6.4 makes it the pilot in command’s responsibility that the useable fuel carried is calculated in accordance with the company fuel policy and is sufficient to complete the flight safely with allowance for deviations. Using the same lines and the same source figures means two pilots given the same flight reach the same numbers.

Units and figures. Part A 6.4.1: all fuel measurements for planning are in pounds; fuel is ordered in litres using the conversion in Part A section 0 (1 L Jet A1 ≈ 1.74 lb). Part A 6.4.2 names the sources of consumption data: the company flight-planning software, FOM Part B (the FCOM) or the manufacturer’s handbook, and FCOM 7.1.1 says its table is to be used when the software is unavailable or for in-flight re-planning — which is what a hand calculation is. Figures in brackets on the sheet are listed in the order Legacy · EX · Blackhawk. The holding rate is the FCOM lb/hr figure ÷ 60 (300 lb/hr → 5 lb per minute; Blackhawk 350 → 5.8); the final reserve, approach allowance and holding rate are printed in the header strip of each panel so nothing needs to be looked up. The cruise burn is deliberately not printed — use the actual fuel flow (flow ÷ 60 = lb per minute), since it changes with level, power and temperature. The FCOM warns the figures do not apply with non-standard power settings.

Fuel check — rows 1 to 6 (Part A 6.4.5.2). The PIC must check fuel at regular intervals — normally top of climb and every 60 minutes in cruise — and before passing a decision point, PNR or critical point, recording it on the nav log. The manual’s standard method has three steps, and the rows follow them. Step 1, compare planned with actual consumption: the fuel on board in row 1 (gauges and totaliser reconciled) is checked against the planned figure on the nav log, and the final reserve is set aside straight away (6.4.3(g): it must still be in the tanks after the final landing), so every figure from here on is fuel you are free to use. Step 2, confirm the fuel is enough to finish the flight: row 2 takes off the burn for the time still to run at the actual fuel flow (flow ÷ 60 = lb per minute) and gives the usable fuel overhead the destination. Step 3, the expected fuel on landing and what it allows: row 3 takes off one approach allowance (6.4.3(c)) and converts the rest to holding minutes at the holding rate — the manual’s step 3(a); row 4 also takes off the fuel needed to reach the alternate (6.4.3(f): missed approach, climb, routing, descent, approach, landing — from the EFB plan) before converting to minutes — step 3(b), the time you can hold before a diversion must begin; row 5 is the number of approaches that may be attempted before the reserve is touched, which the manual also asks for. Row 6 is the line to act on: when the usable fuel overhead is down to one approach, there is one approach left and you land — that is the final reserve plus one approach in the tanks.

Worked example (Legacy, air transport). 900 lb on board → 700 lb usable with the 200 lb reserve set aside; 40 minutes to run at 370 lb/hr (6.2 lb/min) burns 248 lb → 452 lb usable overhead → (452 − 50) ÷ 5 = 80 minutes of holding, then land → with 150 lb needed for the alternate, (452 − 50 − 150) ÷ 5 = 50 minutes of holding before diverting → 452 ÷ 50 = 9 approaches possible → land now once the usable fuel overhead is down to 50 lb.

What is and is not protected. The in-flight check measures holding against the final reserve; contingency fuel is not held back at this stage because it exists for unforeseen factors en route (6.4.3(e)). The exception is additional fuel: where contingency forms part of an additional-fuel calculation it must be intact up to the critical point, and any use of it before the CP requires a recalculation and, if necessary, a diversion (6.4.3(h)).

Insufficient fuel — 6.4.5.2 note and Part A 5.21. If the check shows insufficient fuel to continue as planned, the PIC must re-plan to an available alternate safe landing area; if no option exists that lands with the required reserves intact, a fuel emergency must be declared. Row 6 is the working line: usable fuel overhead down to one approach (final reserve plus one approach in the tanks) means one approach and land; a clearance that would take you below the final reserve is a MINIMUM FUEL declaration; a calculated landing fuel below the final reserve is MAYDAY FUEL.

Top of descent — rows 7 to 10 (FCOM 8.5.3). The FCOM method: the TOD is calculated so the aircraft arrives in the circuit or at the nominated IAF already in the applicable configuration, planned at 500 ft/min for passenger comfort; height to lose is counted in thousands of feet and the distance needed is groundspeed ÷ 30 nm per thousand. The sheet uses the standard company figure of 6 nm per 1,000 ft — the FCOM worked example at 180 kt (180 ÷ 30 = 6; 6,000 ft = 36 nm) — rather than recalculating it each leg; with a lower groundspeed the same distance simply gives a gentler descent, and with a strong tailwind start a little earlier. It is applied to a gate — the altitude you want to be at by a distance from the aerodrome: 1,500 ft by 5 nm for a straight-in (the FCOM 8.5.4 straight-in profile: ~1,500 ft at 5 nm, ~1,000 ft at 3 nm, 300 ft at 1 nm), circuit height by the join point, or the IAF altitude by the IAF. Row 8 takes the height from cruise down to the gate in thousands of feet and multiplies by 6 for the distance that descent needs, and row 9 adds the gate’s own distance to give the TOD as nm from the aerodrome — so the descent is continuous from TOD through the gate to the runway. Example — Palm Island straight-in from 8,000 ft, gate 1,500 ft by 5 nm: 8,000 − 1,500 = 6,500 ft = 6.5 units; 6.5 × 6 = 39 nm; TOD = 39 + 5 = 44 nm from Palm Island. Row 10 is the profile check at any point in the descent: (nm to run − gate nm) ÷ 6 × 1,000 + gate altitude is the altitude you should be at — 20 nm to run in the example gives (20 − 5) ÷ 6 = 2.5 → 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000 ft; high means descend faster or extend, low means shallow out. Set the published company descent torque, reducing it in turbulence to keep the IAS below Va; adjust for terrain, LSALT/MSA, CTA steps, traffic and ATC; descent checks are completed two minutes before TOD (Part A 6.35).

Radio table (AIP ENR 1.1 §6.1; CASA AC 91-35 Fig 22–23). Taxiing and departure are mandatory IFR reports in Class G. The taxi report goes to Centre on the area frequency once you have established contact — callsign, IFR and type, POB, “TAXIES [from] FOR [to]”, the runway, and “REQUEST CLEARANCE” if the route enters controlled airspace — and you read back the squawk and QNH that come back; the same details are broadcast on the CTAF in the “[LOCATION] TRAFFIC” form. The departure report follows “[callsign], DEPARTURE” and ATC’s reply: without surveillance it is the departure time, outbound track, level climbing to and the estimate for the next position; where identification is expected it is distance and direction from the aerodrome, altitude passing, level climbing to and the estimate, and ATC answers IDENTIFIED. Write the nine figures into the leg’s boxes before the call so they are read, not worked out, on the radio; the underlined words in the two scripts under the table are the box headings. A · Minimum fuel at engine start (Part A 6.4.4.1)

  1. Taxi = 35 lb
  2. Trip = leg minutes × cruise lb/min (370 ÷ 60 = 6.2)TRIP = MIN × 6.2Exact: climb minutes at 400 lb/hr. Below 4,000 ft use 400 lb/hr.
  3. Approach = 50 lb for each IAP / STAR / night visual (destination, and the alternate)
  4. Contingency = 10% of trip, never less than 25 lb. Not applied to the alternate.CONT = MAX( TRIP × 0.10 , 25 )
  5. Holding = minutes × holding lb/min (300 ÷ 60 = 5.0)HOLD = MIN × 5.0 → INTER 30 =150 · TEMPO 60 = 300 · CNS/TSV 10 = 50CNS/TSV 10 min is replaced by, not added to, INTER/TEMPO or NOTAM holding.
  6. Alternate (if required) = alternate minutes × 370 ÷ 60 + 50 lb approach (+ any alternate holding). No contingency.
  7. Final reserve = 200 lb (200 Air Transport · 275 non-air-transport). Must still be in the tanks after landing.
  8. Discretionary = PIC's call / client memo
MINIMUM FUEL = TAXI + TRIP + APPROACH + CONTINGENCY + HOLDING + ALTERNATE + RESERVE + DISCRETIONARY
FCOM recommends planning to land with no less than 300 lb.

B · In-flight check (Part A 6.4.5.2)

  1. Fuel overhead = FOB now − (minutes to run × cruise lb/hr ÷ 60)OVERHEAD = FOB − ( MIN × CRUISE ÷ 60 )
  2. Landing fuel = overhead − one approach (50 lb)LANDING = OVERHEAD − 50
  3. Holding available = (landing fuel − reserve) ÷ holding lb/minHOLD MIN = ( LANDING − 200 ) ÷ 5.0
  4. Approaches available = (landing fuel − reserve) ÷ approach fuel, whole numberAPPROACHES = ( LANDING − 200 ) ÷ 50
  5. Holding before diverting = (landing fuel − reserve − alternate) ÷ holding lb/minHOLD MIN = ( LANDING − 200 − ALTERNATE ) ÷ 5.0ALTERNATE = alt minutes × 6.2 + 50 lb approach (+ any alternate holding). Reserve is already counted.
  6. Minimum fuel line = reserve + one approach = 250 lb overhead. At it: one approach, land. Any clearance change that could put you below 200 lb → declare MINIMUM FUEL. Calculated landing fuel below 200 lb → MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY FUEL (Part A 5.21).
