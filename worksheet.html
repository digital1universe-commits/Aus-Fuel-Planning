What the sheet does. It reproduces, line for line, the two fuel procedures every company pilot must follow — the minimum useable fuel calculation before flight (Part A 6.4.4.1) and the in-flight fuel quantity check (Part A 6.4.5.2) — and the FCOM top-of-descent method (8.5.3). Part A 6.4 makes it the pilot in command’s responsibility that the useable fuel carried is calculated in accordance with the company fuel policy and is sufficient to complete the flight safely with allowance for deviations. Using the same lines and the same source figures means two pilots given the same flight reach the same numbers.

Units and figures. Part A 6.4.1: all fuel measurements for planning are in pounds; fuel is ordered in litres using the conversion in Part A section 0 (1 L Jet A1 ≈ 1.74 lb). Part A 6.4.2 names the sources of consumption data: the company flight-planning software, FOM Part B (the FCOM) or the manufacturer’s handbook, and FCOM 7.1.1 says its table is to be used when the software is unavailable or for in-flight re-planning — which is what a hand calculation is. The per-minute figures on the sheet are the FCOM lb/hr figures ÷ 60, listed in the order Legacy · EX · Blackhawk. The FCOM warns the figures do not apply with non-standard power settings.

A1 Taxi — 6.4.3(a). A fixed FCOM figure (35 lb Legacy, 45 lb EX and Blackhawk). Add to it where Part A says to consider longer taxi: ground holding, metering, single-runway operations, de-icing.

A2 Trip — 6.4.3(b). Fuel for take-off and climb to the initial cruise level, cruise, descent and the expected approach procedure, with allowance for ATC delays, re-routing or level changes. The sheet splits it into climb minutes at the climb rate (A2a) and cruise-plus-descent minutes at the cruise rate (A2b — the FCOM gives one figure for cruise and descent); use the low-level figure for legs flown below 4,000 ft.

A3 Approach — 6.4.3(c). Additional fuel for a STAR, an instrument approach, a route-guide IMC arrival or a visual approach at night, at the FCOM company allowance (50 lb; 60 lb Blackhawk). It applies at the destination and again at the alternate (A6). The Part A note asks for further consideration of long outbound legs, overhead arrivals and non-standard arrivals such as the Cairns RWY 33 LOC from the north.

A4 Contingency — 6.4.3(e). The highest of 10% of trip fuel or 5 minutes at holding speed 1,500 ft above the destination; the FCOM expresses the 5 minutes as a minimum of 25 lb (30 lb EX and Blackhawk). It covers unforeseen factors only and is not applied to the alternate fuel.

A5 Holding — 6.4.3(d) and 6.4.3(i). Fuel for a specified time at the holding rate: weather holding when TEMPO or INTER is forecast below the alternate minima (60 or 30 minutes), traffic holding directed by ATC, AIP, ERSA or NOTAM, and aerodrome lighting requirements. Company policy requires 10 minutes of holding fuel inbound to Cairns and Townsville; this is superseded by, not added to, any weather or NOTAM holding.

A6 Alternate — 6.4.3(f). Where an alternate is operationally required: a missed approach at the destination, climb to cruise, the routing to the alternate, descent to the IAF, the approach and the landing — hence minutes at the cruise rate plus one approach allowance, plus any holding required at the alternate. The note asks whether more than one attempt at the destination should be planned before diverting (B6 answers that in flight). No contingency is added.

A7 Final reserve — 6.4.3(g). Fuel to fly at holding speed 1,500 ft above an aerodrome for 30 minutes (turbine, air transport) or 45 minutes (non-air-transport); it must remain in the tanks until the final landing is complete. The FCOM fixed figures (200 / 275 lb; Blackhawk 225 / 310 lb) already include the +50 lb that Part A 6.4.5.1 notes is built into the company reserve for gauge tolerance.

A8–A10 — 6.4.3(h), (i), (j). Additional fuel is the extra needed for the most critical scenario (engine failure, depressurisation) from the critical point — the Part A worked example is for the B200; discretionary fuel is extra at the PIC’s discretion or required by a client memo or route guide; ballast fuel keeps the centre of gravity within limits. Part A 6.4.4.1 lists all of these, with any other extra prescribed by the company, as the components of the minimum useable fuel at engine start — the sheet’s MINIMUM FUEL line.

A11 Landing check. The FCOM 7.1.1 note recommends planning to land with no less than 300 lb on board. The line removes what a normal flight burns (taxi, trip, approach) from the minimum fuel to show the planned landing figure if no holding or contingency is used.

A12 Uplift — 6.4.5.1. Before flight the quantity on board must be checked by two methods — normally the post-flight report figure against the gauges and totaliser. If they agree within ±100 lb (C208) the post-flight report figure is treated as accurate; the lower of the two is used for flight calculations and set on the totaliser, the higher for weight and balance. Extra fuel above the minimum must be shown on the flight plan. Fuel is ordered in litres.

B · In-flight check — 6.4.5.2. The PIC must check fuel quantity at regular intervals — normally at top of climb and every 60 minutes in cruise — and before passing a decision point, PNR or critical point, recording the check on the nav log. The manual’s standard method is the sheet’s three steps: (1) compare planned with actual consumption (B1); (2) determine the useable fuel remaining and that it is sufficient to complete the flight including any holding or alternate (B2); (3) determine the useable fuel expected on landing at the destination (B3), then (a) subtract the final reserve and convert to holding minutes (B4), and (b) subtract the final reserve and the fuel to the nominated alternate and convert to holding minutes — the time available before a diversion must begin (B5). The manual also lists determining the number of approaches that may be attempted (B6). The expected landing fuel is fuel now, less the burn for the time to run at the cruise rate, less the approach allowance.

What is and is not protected. The in-flight check measures holding against the final reserve; contingency fuel is not held back at this stage because it exists for unforeseen factors en route (6.4.3(e)). The exception is additional fuel: where contingency forms part of an additional-fuel calculation it must be intact up to the critical point, and any use of it before the CP requires a recalculation and, if necessary, a diversion (6.4.3(h)).

Insufficient fuel — 6.4.5.2 note and Part A 5.21. If the check shows insufficient fuel to continue as planned, the PIC must re-plan to an available alternate safe landing area; if no option exists that lands with the required reserves intact, a fuel emergency must be declared. B7 is the working line: final reserve plus one approach overhead means one approach and land; a clearance that would take you below the final reserve is a MINIMUM FUEL declaration; a calculated landing fuel below the final reserve is MAYDAY FUEL.

C · Top of descent — FCOM 8.5.3. The TOD is calculated so the aircraft arrives in the circuit or at the nominated IAF in the applicable configuration, planned at 500 ft/min for passenger comfort. Height loss is the cruising altitude less ground level (the FCOM example: 8,000 ft AMSL with an aerodrome at 2,000 ft = 6,000 ft = 6 units); the sheet uses the circuit height or IAF altitude as the target so the aircraft is level and configured before the join. The descent calculation is relative to groundspeed: groundspeed ÷ 30 gives the nm per 1,000 ft (150 kt → 5, 180 kt → 6), so a 6,000 ft descent at 180 kt starts 36 nm out. Descent time is the height loss ÷ 500 ft/min. Set the published company descent torque, reducing it in turbulence to keep the IAS below Va; make adjustments for wind, terrain, LSALT/MSA, the initial approach altitude, CTA steps, traffic and ATC, and monitor ALT/DIST through the descent — C7 gives the checkpoint arithmetic (units remaining × nm per unit). Descent checks are completed two minutes before TOD (Part A 6.35).
