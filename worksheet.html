What the sheet does. It reproduces, line for line, the in-flight fuel quantity check every company pilot must carry out (Part A 6.4.5.2) and the FCOM top-of-descent method (8.5.3). The minimum useable fuel at engine start (Part A 6.4.4.1) is produced by the company flight-planning software (Part A 6.4.2(a)) and is not repeated here — bring the planned FOB, the alternate fuel and the final reserve from the EFB nav log. Part A 6.4 makes it the pilot in command’s responsibility that the useable fuel carried is calculated in accordance with the company fuel policy and is sufficient to complete the flight safely with allowance for deviations. Using the same lines and the same source figures means two pilots given the same flight reach the same numbers.

Units and figures. Part A 6.4.1: all fuel measurements for planning are in pounds; fuel is ordered in litres using the conversion in Part A section 0 (1 L Jet A1 ≈ 1.74 lb). Part A 6.4.2 names the sources of consumption data: the company flight-planning software, FOM Part B (the FCOM) or the manufacturer’s handbook, and FCOM 7.1.1 says its table is to be used when the software is unavailable or for in-flight re-planning — which is what a hand calculation is. The per-minute figures on the sheet are the FCOM lb/hr figures ÷ 60, listed in the order Legacy · EX · Blackhawk; the final reserve, approach allowance and holding rate are printed in the header strip of each panel so nothing needs to be looked up; the cruise burn is deliberately not printed — use the actual fuel flow. The FCOM warns the figures do not apply with non-standard power settings.

Fuel check — rows 1 to 9 (Part A 6.4.5.2). The PIC must check fuel at regular intervals — normally top of climb and every 60 minutes in cruise — and before passing a decision point, PNR or critical point, recording it on the nav log. The manual’s standard method has three steps, and the rows follow them. Step 1, compare planned with actual consumption: row 1 is the reconciled fuel on board, which you compare with the planned figure on the nav log. Row 2 sets the final reserve aside straight away (6.4.3(g): it must still be in the tanks after the final landing), so every figure from here on is fuel you are free to use. Step 2, confirm the fuel is enough to finish the flight: row 3 is the burn for the time still to run at the actual fuel flow, and row 4 is the usable fuel you will have overhead the destination. Step 3, the expected fuel on landing and what it allows: row 5 takes off one approach allowance (6.4.3(c)), leaving the spare fuel after landing; row 6 converts that to holding minutes at the holding rate — the manual’s step 3(a); row 7 first takes out the fuel needed to reach the alternate (6.4.3(f): missed approach, climb, routing, descent, approach, landing — from the EFB plan) and converts the rest to holding minutes — step 3(b), the time you can hold before a diversion must begin; row 8 is the number of approaches that may be attempted, which the manual also asks for. Row 9 is the line to act on: when the usable fuel overhead is down to one approach, there is one approach left and you land — that is the final reserve plus one approach in the tanks.

Worked example (Legacy, air transport). 900 lb on board → 700 lb usable with the 200 lb reserve set aside; 40 minutes to run at 370 lb/hr (6.2 lb/min) burns 248 lb → 452 lb usable overhead → 402 lb spare after one approach → 80 minutes of holding, then land → with 150 lb needed for the alternate, 50 minutes of holding before diverting → 8 approaches possible → land now once the usable fuel overhead is down to 50 lb.

What is and is not protected. The in-flight check measures holding against the final reserve; contingency fuel is not held back at this stage because it exists for unforeseen factors en route (6.4.3(e)). The exception is additional fuel: where contingency forms part of an additional-fuel calculation it must be intact up to the critical point, and any use of it before the CP requires a recalculation and, if necessary, a diversion (6.4.3(h)).

Insufficient fuel — 6.4.5.2 note and Part A 5.21. If the check shows insufficient fuel to continue as planned, the PIC must re-plan to an available alternate safe landing area; if no option exists that lands with the required reserves intact, a fuel emergency must be declared. B7 is the working line: final reserve plus one approach overhead means one approach and land; a clearance that would take you below the final reserve is a MINIMUM FUEL declaration; a calculated landing fuel below the final reserve is MAYDAY FUEL.

Top of descent — rows 10 to 15 (FCOM 8.5.3). The FCOM method: the TOD is calculated so the aircraft arrives in the circuit or at the nominated IAF already in the applicable configuration, planned at 500 ft/min for passenger comfort; height to lose is counted in thousands of feet and the distance needed is groundspeed ÷ 30 nm per thousand. The sheet uses the standard company figure of 6 nm per 1,000 ft — the FCOM worked example at 180 kt (180 ÷ 30 = 6; 6,000 ft = 36 nm) — rather than recalculating it each leg; with a lower groundspeed the same distance simply gives a gentler descent, and with a strong tailwind start a little earlier. It is applied to a gate — the altitude you want to be at by a distance from the aerodrome: 1,500 ft by 5 nm for a straight-in (the FCOM 8.5.4 straight-in profile: ~1,500 ft at 5 nm, ~1,000 ft at 3 nm, 300 ft at 1 nm), circuit height by the join point, or the IAF altitude by the IAF. Row 12 is the height from cruise down to the gate, row 13 the distance that descent needs at 6 nm per thousand, and row 14 adds the gate’s own distance to give the TOD as nm from the aerodrome — so the descent is continuous from TOD through the gate to the runway. Example — Palm Island straight-in from 8,000 ft, gate 1,500 ft by 5 nm: 8,000 − 1,500 = 6,500 ft = 6.5 units; 6.5 × 6 = 39 nm; TOD = 39 + 5 = 44 nm from Palm Island. Row 15 is the profile check at any point in the descent: (nm to run − gate nm) ÷ 6 × 1,000 + gate altitude is the altitude you should be at — 20 nm to run in the example gives (20 − 5) ÷ 6 = 2.5 → 2,500 + 1,500 = 4,000 ft; high means descend faster or extend, low means shallow out. Set the published company descent torque, reducing it in turbulence to keep the IAS below Va; adjust for terrain, LSALT/MSA, CTA steps, traffic and ATC; descent checks are completed two minutes before TOD (Part A 6.35).
